title: "What Can We Learn by Reading a Tumor’s DNA?"
---

If researchers find 100 mutations in a tumor, how do they figure out which ones helped it grow? Genomic sequencing gives them a way to investigate that question. By reading the DNA from a tumor and comparing it with DNA from healthy cells from the same patient, researchers can identify changes that the tumor cells acquired. They can then compare tumors from different patients to see which genes are repeatedly affected.

### Which DNA changes matter?

People naturally differ at individual DNA letters. For example, at one position in the genome, some people might have an A while others have a G. A common single-letter difference like this is called a **single nucleotide polymorphism**, or **SNP** (pronounced “snip”). If researchers find the same variant in a patient’s tumor and healthy cells, it may be an inherited part of that person’s DNA. A change found only in the tumor sample is more likely to be a **somatic mutation**, meaning it arose in a cell during the patient’s lifetime.

What stood out to me was how few of a tumor’s mutations may actually help it grow. In their 2013 paper *Cancer Genome Landscapes*, Vogelstein and colleagues estimated that a typical tumor contained **two to eight driver gene mutations**. A **driver gene** is a gene in which certain mutations give a cell a growth or survival advantage. As that cell divides, its descendants can become a larger share of the tumor. Other changes, called **passenger mutations**, get copied along with the drivers even though they give the cell no growth advantage of their own.

### How can a mutation drive cancer?

One example is **KRAS**, a gene that normally helps cells respond to signals telling them when to grow. Certain mutations can leave the KRAS protein switched on, so the cell keeps receiving a growth signal. In this altered form, KRAS acts as an **oncogene**, a gene whose activation can promote cancer. A mutation can also give a cell an advantage by disabling a **tumor suppressor gene**. For example, losing the function of **TP53** can allow a cell with damaged DNA to keep dividing.

Another lesson from sequencing is that two tumors can have different mutations but disrupt the same cellular process. As Garraway and Lander discussed in *Lessons from the Cancer Genome*, researchers found cancer-related changes in processes ranging from cell signaling to how cells process RNA. That changed how I think about a tumor’s mutation list. Knowing which DNA letter changed is a starting point. Understanding what that change lets a cell do helps explain why the tumor grew.
