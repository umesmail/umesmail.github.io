Colon cancer is one of the most common cancers in the United States. In 2026, approximately 108,860 new cases of colon cancer are expected to be diagnosed, including 55,410 cases in men and 53,450 cases in women. Colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed cancer in both men and women in the United States, excluding skin cancers (1). Colon cancer is typically identified using a colonoscopy, in which a long, flexible tube with a video camera is used to examine the inside of the colon and rectum. During the procedure, a doctor can remove polyps and collect suspicious tissue. A biopsy of the tissue is then examined in a laboratory to determine whether the cells are cancerous. Blood tests are not used to diagnose colon cancer, although they may provide additional information about a patient's health and the cancer (2).

Several factors can increase a person's risk of developing colorectal cancer. Risk factors that can be changed include excess body weight, type 2 diabetes, diets high in red or processed meats, smoking, and alcohol use. Risk also increases with age and is much more common after age 50. Other risk factors include a personal history of colorectal polyps or cancer, inflammatory bowel disease such as Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, previous radiation to the abdomen or pelvis, and a family history of colorectal cancer or adenomatous polyps. Certain inherited conditions can also greatly increase risk, particularly Lynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) (3).

Colorectal cancer is staged from stage 0 through stage IV based on how far the cancer has grown or spread. Stage 0 is the earliest stage, where the cancer has not grown beyond the inner layer of the colon or rectum. Stage I involves growth into deeper layers of the colon or rectum without spreading to lymph nodes or distant sites. Stage II involves further growth through the colon or rectal wall but no spread to nearby lymph nodes or distant sites. Stage III occurs when the cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes but not to distant sites. Stage IV is the most advanced stage and occurs when the cancer has spread to distant areas, such as the liver, lungs, distant lymph nodes, or peritoneum. The American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM system is used to determine these stages based on the tumor's extent (T), spread to nearby lymph nodes (N), and spread to distant sites (M). (4)

Current treatments for colon cancer include surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. The treatment chosen depends on the stage of the cancer. Early-stage colon cancer is primarily treated with surgery, while more advanced stages may require chemotherapy, radiation therapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy in addition to surgery. (5) Life expectancy for colon cancer varies greatly depending on how far the cancer has spread. Rather than giving a specific average number of years, survival is commonly reported using 5-year relative survival rates. For colon cancer, the 5-year relative survival rate is 91% when localized, 74% when regional, and 13% when the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body. Across all stages combined, the 5-year relative survival rate is 63%. These rates are estimates and cannot predict exactly how long an individual patient will live. (6)
